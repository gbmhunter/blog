Optical isolators (a.k.a. opto-isolators or optocouplers) are used to galvanically isolate two circuits while allowing data to pass between them. They are used to prevent high voltages from damaging sensitive electronics, prevent noise from one part of a circuit from affecting another part, and to communicate across circuits which must be isolated for safety reasons.

Types:

- Phototransistor optocouplers: A [phototransistor](/electronics/components/sensors/phototransistors/) is used to drive the output.
- Phototriac optocouplers.

Opto-isolators are similar to [relays](/electronics/components/relays/). The key differences are:

- Relays are typically used for turning on and off high current loads, while opto-isolators are used for communication (i.e. signals/low current).
- Relays have been traditionally electro-mechanical (although solid-state relays are available), while opto-isolators have always generally been solid-state.

Many opto-isolators use an infrared emitting diode optically coupled to a phototransistor, all within a single enclosed component.

AC capable optocouplers usually have two LEDs in anti-parallel.

### The Current-Transfer Ratio (CTR)

The _current-transfer ratio_ (CTR) of a BJT-based optical isolator tells you how well it amplifies an input signal to an output signal. It is the ratio of the collector current $I_C$ at the output side relative to the forward current $I_f$ going through the [LED](/electronics/components/diodes/light-emitting-diodes-leds/) on the input side, and then expressed as a percentage[^wurth-ano007-understanding-phototransistor-optocouplers]:

$$
\begin{align*}
CTR = \frac{I_C}{I_f} \cdot 100\%
\end{align*}
$$

For optical isolators with a single phototransistor driver, the current-transfer ratio is normally in the 20-400% range. Sometimes within a family there are a multitude of parts with different transfer ratios, specified by slightly different part numbers. The EL3H7-G series from Everlight is one such example.

<Image src={import('./_assets/ctr-binning-table-wurth-wl-ocpt816-optocouplers.webp')} width="500px">A table showing the current-transfer ratio (CTR) binning for the Wurth WL-OCPT816 series of optocouplers[^wurth-ano007-understanding-phototransistor-optocouplers].</Image>

The current transfer ratio is specified at a particular forward current $I_f$ through the diode and particular voltage across the output collector-emitter $V_{CE}$.

### Bidirectional Phototransistor Opto-isolators

Opto-isolators can be made to support bidirectional inputs by adding a second photodiode in anti-parallel on the input side. This makes the opto-isolator turn on to both positive and negative input signals.

The schematic symbol for a bidirectional phototransistor opto-isolator is shown below:

<Image src={import('./_assets/bidirectional-phototransistor-optocoupler-schematic-symbol.webp')} width="500px">A schematic symbol for a bidirectional phototransistor opto-isolator.</Image>

Bidirectional opto-isolators are used often for AC. One example is the HCPL-354 by Avago.

### Phototransistor Optocoupler with a Base Pin

Some phototransistor-based optocouplers provide an electrical pin connected to the base of the phototransistor. With this pin, you can reduce the turn-off time of the optocoupler. To get the phototransistor to turn off quickly, you need to remove the charge stored in the base-emitter junction. By adding a resistor in the approx. range of $220\unit{k\Omega}$ to $470\unit{k\Omega}$ you can remove this charge quicker than it would naturally and increase the maximum speed the optocoupler can operate at[^eevblog-purpose-of-base-in-optocoupler].

<Image src={import('./_assets/4n25-optocoupler-pinout-with-base-pin.webp')} width="400px">The pinout of a 4N25 optocoupler which contains a pin connected to the base of the phototransistor[^vishay-4n25-optocoupler-ds].</Image>

The downside is that adding a resistor here also reduces the CTR of the optocoupler[^eevblog-purpose-of-base-in-optocoupler][^edaboard-floating-base-pin-of-optocoupler].

Another purpose of the base pin connection is to **improve noise immunity**. By connecting a capacitor between the base and the emitter, you can create a low-pass filter to improve the rejection of high-frequency noise/spikes. A $0.1\unit{nF}$ capacitor is a good starting point[^stack-exchange-optocoupler-with-base-lead].

## Photodiode Optocouplers

Rather than use phototransistors for the receiver, some optocouplers use a photodiode and an amplifier. Photodiodes respond faster than phototransistors and therefore allow for faster transmission speeds. The downside of a photodiode is that it outputs far less current, so an amplifier is needed to boost the signal.

<Aside type="note">
But wait, isn't a photodiode + transistor just the same thing as a phototransistor? How does it make it faster? The key is that in a phototransistor, the large light-collecting junction _is_ the collector-base junction, so its capacitance sits across the output voltage swing and is multiplied by the Miller effect. With a separate photodiode, that capacitance sees an almost constant voltage, and the transistor can be made small with little capacitance of its own. Hewlett-Packard state that separating the photodiode bias and output transistor collector connections increases the speed up to a hundred times that of a conventional phototransistor optocoupler, by reducing the base-collector capacitance.[^hp-single-channel-high-speed-optocouplers]
</Aside>

Some photodiode optocouplers just drive a single transistor (e.g. the 6N135/6N136), others drive a high-gain amplifier and logic gate output (e.g. the 6N137, a.k.a. a _logic gate output optocoupler_), and some add a Schmitt trigger for a clean digital output.

### Examples

#### 6N13x Series

The 6N13x series started as Hewlett-Packard parts.[^hp-single-channel-high-speed-optocouplers] The `6N` prefix comes from the JEDEC registered-device numbering scheme (1N for diodes, 2N for transistors, 4N/6N for optocouplers).

- `6N135`: Photodiode drives an open-collector transistor at speeds up to 1 Mbps. Has a minimum CTR of 7% at $I_F = 16\unit{mA}$.[^hp-single-channel-high-speed-optocouplers]
- `6N136`: Similar to the `6N135`, but with a higher minimum CTR of 19% at $I_F = 16\unit{mA}$.[^hp-single-channel-high-speed-optocouplers]
- `6N137`: A logic gate output optocoupler containing a photodiode, amplifier and logic output, rated at 10 Mbps.[^onsemi-6n137-high-speed-logic-gate-output-optocoupler] The onsemi version is now obsolete.

As of 2026 these parts are still sold by Broadcom, with other manufacturers making compatible parts.[^broadcom-6n135-product-page]

## Phototriac Output Optocouplers

_Phototriac output optocouplers_ (a.k.a. _triac couplers_) are like phototransistor optocouplers, except they have a [triac](/electronics/components/triacs/) instead of a transistor on the output. The triac's gate is controlled by the incoming light source. Phototriac optocouplers are used to switch on and off an AC load in an isolated manner.

<Image src={import('./_assets/phototriac-based-optical-isolator-internal-schematic.png')} width="400px">The internal schematic of an optical isolator with a phototriac output driver rather than a phototransistor. Image from http://www.ti.com/.</Image>

The outputs of phototriac optical isolators usually go on to drive power TRIACs which switch a mains powered load.

## Component Packages

To achieve good isolation, different-net pins must be kept well away from each other to prevent arcing. This prevents many small-pitch packages from being used for optical isolators.

Some of the common packages used for optical isolation components are:

- The through-hole [DIP package](/pcb-design/component-packages/dip-component-package/)
- The surface-mount [SOIC package](/pcb-design/component-packages/soic-component-package/).
- The surface-mount [SOP-4 package](/pcb-design/component-packages/sop-component-package/#sop-4).
