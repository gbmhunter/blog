_Voltage-to-current converters_ are circuits which **convert an input voltage into an output current**. The output current is usually proportional to the input voltage, over some suitable range.

Many of these circuit designs use op-amps. Please see the [Op-Amp page](/electronics/components/op-amps) for more general information on operational amplifiers.

## Howland Current Source

The Howland current source is an op-amp based constant current sink/source. The basic Howland current source schematic is shown below.

### How It Works

First, we'll annotate the schematic with some labels:

<Image src={import('./_assets/howland-current-source-basic-annotated.svg')} width="400px">The basic Howland current source schematic, annotated with labels.</Image>

Appling Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) and Ohm's Law to the circuit, we get:

$$
\begin{align*}
I_L &= I_1 + I_2 \\
    &= \frac{V_I - V_L}{R_1} + \frac{V_A - V_L}{R_2}
\end{align*}
$$

We also can form a relationship between the voltage at the op-amps non-inverting input $V_L$ and the op-amps output voltage $V_A$. We can see that it forms a non-inverting amplifier with a gain of $1 + R_4 / R_3$:

$$
V_A = V_L \left(1 + \frac{R_4}{R_3}\right)
$$

Now we substitute the expression for $V_A$ directly into the KCL equation and simplify. Notice that in the $R_2$ term the $V_L$ contributions cancel, leaving just $V_L \frac{R_4}{R_3}$, after which we group the $V_L$ terms:

$$
\begin{align*}
I_L &= \frac{V_I - V_L}{R_1} + \frac{V_A - V_L}{R_2} \\
    &= \frac{V_I - V_L}{R_1} + \frac{V_L \left(1 + \frac{R_4}{R_3}\right) - V_L}{R_2} \\
    &= \frac{V_I - V_L}{R_1} + \frac{V_L \frac{R_4}{R_3}}{R_2} \\
    &= \frac{V_I}{R_1} - \frac{V_L}{R_1} + \frac{V_L R_4}{R_2 R_3} \\
    &= \frac{V_I}{R_1} - V_L \left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{R_4}{R_2 R_3}\right)
\end{align*}
$$

This separates the output current into a driven term and a load-dependent term:

$$
I_L = A V_I - \frac{V_L}{R_o}
$$

where $A = \frac{1}{R_1}$ is the transconductance gain and $R_o$ is the output resistance the circuit presents to the load:

$$
R_o = \left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{R_4}{R_2 R_3}\right)^{-1} = \frac{R_1 R_2 R_3}{R_2 R_3 - R_1 R_4}
$$

The trick here is to realize we can make the load current independent of the load voltage $V_L$ by driving $R_o$ to infinity (this drives the term $-\frac{V_L}{R_o} \to 0$). This requires balancing the bridge so that the denominator $R_2 R_3 - R_1 R_4$ goes to zero.

The easy way to do this is to make $R_1 = R_3$ and $R_2 = R_4$, which satisfies $R_2 R_3 = R_1 R_4$ automatically:

$$
R_1 = R_3, \quad R_2 = R_4
$$

### Setting The Output Current

With the bridge balanced, the load-dependent term drops out and the output current depends only on the input voltage and $R_1$:

$$
I_L = A V_I = \frac{V_I}{R_1}
$$

So there are two knobs for setting the current. $R_1$ fixes the transconductance gain $A = \frac{1}{R_1}$ (the amps-per-volt), and the input voltage $V_I$ then sets the operating point along that slope. Note that $R_2$ (and its match $R_4$) does not appear in this expression — once the bridge is balanced it affects the output compliance and impedance, but not the current.

For example, to source $20\unit{mA}$ from a $10\unit{V}$ input, we need:

$$
R_1 = \frac{V_I}{I_L} = \frac{10\unit{V}}{20\unit{mA}} = 500\unit{\Omega}
$$

and, to keep the bridge balanced, $R_3 = R_1 = 500\unit{\Omega}$. Keep in mind that the op-amp output $V_A$ must stay within its linear range — if the load voltage $V_L$ climbs high enough to saturate the op-amp, the source can no longer maintain the set current.

The Howland current source is commonly used to build a [4-20mA current-loop transmitter](/electronics/communication-protocols/4-20ma-current-loops).

### Weaknesses

One weaknesses of the basic Howland current source is that it is sensitive to resistor tolerances in the balanced bridge. If the resistors are not well matched, the output current will depend on the load voltage $V_L$ and the output resistance $R_o$ will be finite. This can be mitigated by using precision resistors or trimming the resistors to balance the bridge.[^ti-an-1515-comprehensive-study-of-the-howland-current-pump]

### History

The "Basic Howland Current Pump" was invented by Prof. Bradford Howland in 1962 when working for MIT. It was first published in January 1964 in the "Lightning Empiricist", Volume 12, Number 1.[^ti-an-1515-comprehensive-study-of-the-howland-current-pump] [^philbrick-lightning-empiricist-v12-n1]

## Pre-built ICs

### XTR111

The XTR111 is a current-loop transmitter by Texas Instruments. It can drive grounded loads. It requires both an external BJT and MOSFET. As of May 2016, this IC was US\$1.28 (100).

### XTR117

The XTR117 is a current-loop transmitter by Texas Instruments. It cannot drive grounded loads. It only requires an external MOSFET. As of May 2016, this IC was US\$1.77 (100).
